# Chapter 1 — The Hebrew Roots Version of the Book of Revelation: Textual Authority

Nearly all modern Bible translations in circulation, whether grounded in dominant Greek manuscripts, the Latin Vulgate, or successive revised editions, inherit the textual framework and interpretive paradigm standardized by the early Greek Church. At most major theological junctures, mainstream translations follow readings and interpretive traditions solidified throughout ecclesiastical history, forming a cohesive interpretive thread spanning more than a millennium. ^PFP-00072

Among all available versions, only the Book of Revelation in the HRV (Hebrew Roots Version) descends from an independent ancient textual tradition, separate from every mainstream rendering. It was never absorbed into the unified later ecclesiastical revision system and preserves the primitive form of the text closest to the apostolic age. ^PFP-00073

This chapter lays out a foundational textual‑critical principle for the whole work and corrects a widespread misunderstanding: a late date of manuscript discovery or published release does not prove that a text is a later alteration, forgery, or subjective commentary. The core standard of textual criticism is not the chronology of excavation or printing, but whether a manuscript preserves ancient primitive readings that were altered, suppressed, and standardized within the dominant transmission stream. This is the fundamental criterion for judging a text’s originality and authority. ^PFP-00074

## I. Three Key Variant Readings Confirm That the HRV Preserves an Uncorrupted Ancient Primitive Text

Three passages central to core doctrine, personal distinctions within the Godhead, and eschatological chronology have been standardized in Greek, Latin, and all modern mainstream translations, bearing clear marks of later ecclesiastical doctrinal revision. The HRV alone retains the unique ancient wording for these verses. These variants reinforce one another and form a logically consistent whole, demonstrating that the HRV is not a later human modification but an independently sustained primitive textual tradition. ^PFP-00075

### (1) Revelation 1:10 — Textual Correction on the Sabbath‑Related Crucial Passage

Uniform mainstream rendering: The Lord’s Day Ancient Hebrew reading preserved in the HRV: the first day of the week ^PFP-00076

This is far more than a difference in phrasing. It constitutes core textual evidence touching on the Sabbath commandment, the history of ecclesiastical sacred seasons, and later doctrinal modification. It overturns the ecclesiastical tradition claiming “the Lord’s Day is intrinsic to Scripture” and restores the wording as it stood in the apostolic text. ^PFP-00077

### (2) Revelation 13:1 — Defining the Singular / Plural Blasphemous Name of the Beast

Most modern mainstream translations: blasphemous names (plural) Primitive reading in the HRV ancient base text: the name of blasphemy (singular) ^PFP-00078

The singular noun onoma is attested in early ancient Greek manuscripts and was retained in the classic KJV. The plural onomata comes chiefly from the Alexandrian and Vatican manuscripts and the Latin Vulgate; nearly all later modern translations adopted this plural form as the standard reading. ^PFP-00079

Textual transmission logic reveals which reading is primitive. The vision explicitly describes a beast with seven heads. Later scribes and revisers, naturally influenced by the visual image, would readily harmonize the text into the plural “blasphemous names”, assuming seven heads correspond to seven separate names. This kind of harmonizing adjustment is very common in manuscript transmission. By contrast, retaining a single unified blasphemous name despite the seven‑head vision runs against intuitive interpretation, making deliberate later invention extremely unlikely. ^PFP-00080

This singular reading carries decisive theological weight: the seven heads of the beast are not seven hostile authorities each bearing its own name, but seven successive political phases of one single Roman beast system, sharing one unifying blasphemous identity across all stages. This aligns perfectly with the interpretive framework of this book: this single, enduring blasphemous name is the doctrine of the Trinity, formally codified by the Catholic Church. ^PFP-00081

### (3) Revelation 16:15 — The Person of the Messiah and the Chronology of His Coming

Uniform mainstream rendering: Behold, I come as a thief Ancient Hebrew reading preserved in the HRV: Behold, He comes as a thief ^PFP-00082

These three divergences are not minor grammatical tweaks. They strike at the theological architecture of the Book of Revelation and govern four critical interpretive outcomes: first, the identity of the speaking theological subject; second, the definition of the single blasphemous marker; third, the complete chain of end‑time chronology; fourth, the subordinate personal order between the Father and the Messiah. The standardized revisions in mainstream translations blur the Father‑Son order inherent in Scripture and break the precise end‑times timeline; the ancient HRV text safeguards the original theological boundaries and chronological logic of the passage. ^PFP-00083

## II. Probabilistic Argument from Textual Transmission: Later Writers Could Not Have Invented the HRV’s Unique Ancient Readings

Objective rules of textual transmission and probability dispel the claim that the HRV is a later rewriting or subjectively interpreted production. If the HRV had been altered afterward to fit a private theological agenda, its translators would naturally follow the millennium‑old mainstream ecclesiastical consensus and align with common assumptions. They would have no reason to revise three theologically pivotal passages against the prevailing tradition: ^PFP-00084

Ecclesiastical translations for over a millennium read “the Lord’s Day”. A later translator would have no motive to change it to “the first day of the week” and directly contradict a long‑standing church tradition. ^PFP-00085

The seven‑headed vision naturally inclines readers toward a plural reading of blasphemous names. A reviser would gain nothing by switching to the singular and inviting interpretive controversy. ^PFP-00086

Every received version uses the first‑person “I come”. A later translator would have no incentive to shift it to the third‑person “He comes” and disrupt the familiar scriptural pattern. ^PFP-00087

A further decisive point: the translators of the HRV themselves did not possess the full eschatological timeline and anti‑Trinitarian framework developed in this book. They could not have foreseen the chronological tension between the Messiah’s coming in Revelation 14 and Revelation 16, nor deliberately alter pronouns to resolve that tension. Nor would they adjust noun number merely to argue that the Trinity is the single blasphemous name. ^PFP-00088

The plain fact is this: the HRV translators faithfully rendered an ancient Hebrew base text before them, without subjective tampering or interpretive manipulation to suit a private theological system. All its uncommon, counter‑traditional readings originate in the ancient source text itself, not from the translator’s own composition. ^PFP-00089

## III. Historical Origin: The HRV Is a Pure Independent Tradition Uncontaminated by the Ecclesiastical System

After the fourth century, the Greek Church completed the standardization of Scripture and established the doctrine of the Trinity as official orthodoxy. All primitive readings and Hebrew transmission streams conflicting with this orthodoxy were systematically suppressed, rewritten, and assimilated. From that time onward, Greek, Latin, and all derivative translations fell under this centralized ecclesiastical revision scheme. Key verses were adjusted according to official theological standards, erasing the purity of the primitive text. ^PFP-00090

Nearly all ancient traditions were absorbed and corrupted in this way. The Hebrew textual tradition behind the HRV is one of the rare surviving pure lines, marked by four irreplaceable characteristics: ^PFP-00091

- Its transmission was never assimilated into the theological system of the Greek Church;
- It did not follow later mainstream revisions to key subjects, core nouns, or noun number;
- It preserves apostolic‑age primitive readings predating the fourth‑century ecclesiastical standardization;
- At these three doctrinally critical points, it avoids the ecclesiastical alterations found elsewhere and retains the original theological order.

^PFP-00092

We therefore reach a definitive conclusion: the Hebrew Book of Revelation in the HRV is not a later rewrite or fabrication. It is a rare, nearly unique pure Hebrew tradition, untouched by the Trinitarian institutional framework and unmodified by later ecclesiastical editing. It preserves the apostolic original form. ^PFP-00093
