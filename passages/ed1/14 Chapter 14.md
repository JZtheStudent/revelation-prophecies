# Chapter 14 — The Fulfillment of the Law

(These are independent interpretive reflections and do not represent mainstream church consensus. Continuing the preceding chapters, this chapter examines the mainstream misreading of “the fulfillment of the law” based on the Messiah’s own teaching, and clarifies the relationship between grace and the law.) ^PFP-00383

The Old Testament foretold the mission of this Servant, the Messiah: Yehowah is well pleased, for His righteousness’ sake, to magnify the law and make it honourable (Isaiah 42:21). He came not to dilute or annul the law, but to exalt and honour it. This prophecy finds its perfect fulfilment in Yeshua’s Sermon on the Mount. ^PFP-00384

In the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua states plainly that He came not to abolish the law and the prophets, but to fulfil them (Matthew 5:17). He did not remove the demands of the law from humanity. Instead, He carried the standard of the law from outward, flesh‑level observance of written rules inward to the motives, thoughts and attitudes of the heart. According to Yeshua’s teaching, merely refraining from murder or adultery is insufficient. Anger and lust within the heart likewise transgress the essence of the law before Yehowah. He raised the spiritual standard of the law, yet declared at the same time: heaven and earth may pass away, but one jot or one tittle shall in no wise pass from the law (Matthew 5:18). No commandment is trivial. Whoever breaks even the least of these commandments and teaches others to do so shall be called least in the kingdom of heaven. This confirms that faithfulness to the commandments remains an ongoing requirement. ^PFP-00385

Mainstream Christianity verbally affirms Yeshua’s words “I came not to abolish the law, but to fulfil it”, yet has settled on a critical misreading of this phrase. “Fulfilment” is taken to mean that Yeshua once and for all satisfied every requirement of the law on the cross, so believers no longer need to keep the law themselves. Since Yeshua fulfilled it for us, we are released from obedience to the commandments. This interpretation clashes sharply with Isaiah’s prophecy and the immediate context of the Sermon on the Mount. Right after saying He came to fulfil the law, Yeshua proclaims that heaven and earth may pass away, yet not the smallest part of the law; and He directly ties keeping the commandments and teaching others to keep them to one’s standing in the kingdom. His fulfilment is not keeping the law in our place so as to cancel our duty of obedience. Rather, it reveals the true intent of the law, brings its demands into the heart, lifts its spiritual standard, and calls us to obey from within. This fulfils Isaiah’s promise that the law would be magnified and honoured. ^PFP-00386

Yeshua defines the threshold of the kingdom further in Matthew 7:21–23: not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven. In the day of judgment, many will plead their case: they have preached in His name, cast out demons in His name, and worked mighty works in His name. The Lord will say to them: “I never knew you; depart from me, ye that work ἀνομία.” The Greek word ἀνομία (anomia) means lawlessness, disregard for the law. Most Chinese translations render it simply “workers of iniquity”, which easily misleads. Common “iniquity” may be judged by human subjective morality, but the benchmark for ἀνομία is Yehowah’s law. Yeshua rejects them not because they committed worldly evil deeds, but because despite their impressive religious ministry, they were lawless. ^PFP-00387

At the close of the Sermon on the Mount, Yeshua reinforces this with the parable of the rock and the sand: whosoever heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them, shall be likened unto a wise man, which built his house upon a rock. The rain descended, the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell not: for it was founded upon a rock. And every one that heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them not, shall be likened unto a foolish man, which built his house upon the sand. The rain descended, the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house; and it fell: and great was the fall of it (Matthew 7:24–27). This parable places doing the word at the centre. Merely hearing, agreeing, or even preaching Yeshua’s teaching is not enough; obedience is the foundation of life. It follows that Yeshua’s teaching is not only receiving knowledge or a verbal confession, but carrying out obedience in action. What He commands us to do is the law of God which He has fulfilled and elevated: aligning heart and conduct with the Father’s commandments. Hearing without doing gives faith no stable foundation. Only hearing and doing establishes life on an unshakable rock. This echoes His judgment upon those in ἀνομία: the test is not whether one performs spiritual works in the Lord’s name, but whether one obeys His words and the law of God. ^PFP-00388

The reason mainstream churches later adopted the practice that New‑Testament believers need not keep the Mosaic law springs from a particular reading of Paul’s letters. Paul is read as emphasising justification by faith alone, placing believers “not under the law, but under grace”. Within this framework, Paul’s “law of Christ” is separated from the law Yehowah gave at Sinai and written on stone tablets. In 2 Corinthians 3, Paul contrasts the ministry of Moses with the ministry of Christ. Mainstream exegesis treats the commandment system on stone as a ministry of condemnation and curse, possessing only temporary glory, while the gospel of Christ brings justification and glory that fades not. From this premise, many conclude that returning to keep the Mosaic law is a departure from grace; justification comes by faith alone, apart from law‑keeping. This teaching extends into a dangerous logic: once a person enters grace by faith, he is no longer under the law’s judgment. Even if he continues sinning, his sin is not counted against him, and he is declared righteous before God. ^PFP-00389

This conclusion stands as a serious deviation from Isaiah’s prophecy and Yeshua’s own teaching in the Sermon on the Mount. The work of the cross bore the penalty for transgressions of the law, provides forgiveness and grace, and grants power to obey. It does not cancel the law’s requirements for believers, nor does it replace the obedient life we are called to live. Grace is not a licence to sin without judgment, nor a status whereby one remains counted righteous while persistently breaking the commandments. Grace and heartfelt obedience to the law are not opposites. Grace empowers one to fulfil and magnify the law; it does not abolish the standard or exempt us from obedience. To treat grace as release from the law, so that sin is no longer reckoned, is to lead people toward licence and is a false guide. ^PFP-00390
